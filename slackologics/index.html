Around 5:30 on Sunday morning, I moved the remaining Helix work to Claude on Asmit's laptop. Codex was running into its usage quota. We had an application to finish and a submission deadline approaching.

Claude read the Slack history, inspected the checkout, and came back with a problem: most of the implementation described in our updates wasn't on that machine.

It was on mine. Four local commits, plus uncommitted Finance Lab and physical-profile work. The newest Helix branch available remotely still stopped at Stage 1.

We had a detailed shared conversation about a product that the next coding session could not yet access.

The code was recoverable. We checked the branches, secured the outstanding changes, pushed the right feature branch, and fetched it into a separate worktree on Asmit's laptop. By 5:40, the new session was wiring up the report and financial handoff.

Getting there involved considerably more than connecting a few AI tools to Slack.

FRIDAY: GET EVERYONE IN THE ROOM

At 7:59 Friday evening, I posted that I had ChatGPT connected and asked whether we could get Claude in too. Two minutes later, Vince, my ChatGPT, introduced itself.

Its first useful clarification was practical: mentions did not automatically wake it up. I still had to ask it to check Slack.

Then I noticed its messages were being sent through my account.

That distinction followed us through the weekend. A message under my name might be something I typed, a report from Codex, or Vince relaying a decision. Asmit and Jayant's Claude sessions also posted through their accounts. We started using labels such as agent, owner, branch, and next step to make the authorship clearer.

Slack was useful to the humans for another reason. We were in a quiet environment, and I wanted most of our communication written there rather than spoken across the room. The agents could then read the same discussion instead of receiving three separate retellings.

There were some early limits to the arrangement. When Jayant asked Kiro for a summary, it returned a detailed account of his NFL Big Data Bowl project. Receivers, cornering metrics, route tracking. We were trying to build a commercial-property product.

The bot was reachable. It just had the wrong project context.

We also started with a Slack canvas for the specification and decisions, and a task-board list. Codex reported that it couldn't edit the board. Within minutes, Asmit's Claude announced a simpler arrangement: tasks would be messages in #tasks.

One message per task. An owner. A branch. A reaction for in progress, done, or blocked. Updates and questions in the thread. Longer session reports in #agent-log.

The rule in our agent instructions was straightforward: a decision made in a private AI chat had to be written into the shared record.

That gave us a way to hand work around. It still required us to read what had been written.

FRIDAY NIGHT: AGREE ON WHAT WE ARE BUILDING

My idea was to start with an existing building and investigate what it could become. The sponsor material gave us CoStar property records, Matterport interiors, and Vexcel aerial imagery. A warehouse could have uses beyond remaining a warehouse. We wanted to investigate those possibilities and what it would cost to pursue them.

Asmit was researching the businesses and the data. He proposed a building-evidence ledger: compare what the property records said with what the interior and exterior evidence showed, then develop different applications for lenders, insurers, or owners.

Jayant and his assistants challenged the pitch. A generic report combining the sponsor's three sources would be an obvious entry. They wanted a specific finding, a convincing customer, and something worth showing in a short demo.

Some of that criticism was useful. Some of it was aimed at a different product.

At 10:32, after another critique of the due-diligence-report idea, I wrote: “both of your agents misunderstood the pitch.”

I meant redevelopment: what the space could become. The diligence supported that decision. I hadn't proposed diligence as the entire product.

The corrections were recorded. Jayant's Claude and Codex both acknowledged that their earlier assessments had shifted the emphasis away from redevelopment. Other ideas remained in the conversation, including business-continuity planning and Asmit's lender-facing evidence ledger.

The disagreement took time, but it also produced work we eventually needed. Asmit tested whether the proposed contradiction checks found anything real. Jayant explored Capital One's Nessie sandbox. Codex built a small browser playground over the sponsor data. Vince researched public-data coverage and worked through a manual property case.

We could compare results instead of continuing to debate a pitch in the abstract.

SATURDAY MORNING: GET THE PIECES TO AGREE

The first property playground pulled records, room metadata, and an aerial image for 9700 Tanner Road in Houston.

That sounded like a successful three-source match. Looking more closely exposed the complication: the linked Matterport scan was titled 9750 Tanner Road. Its warehouse height field was zero, which could not responsibly become a measured clear height.

Asmit's data pilot found another useful distinction. An apparently empty suite and a tenant record could describe different moments in time. An older image was not automatically evidence that a newer tenancy was false. Later reconciliation rules also qualified some early “confirmed contradiction” claims.

Those details affected how we built the system. The work needed an evidence format that could preserve a source, its scope, a date when available, and an explicit unknown. Jayant's Claude proposed the record shape and repeatedly asked for the UI to confirm it before integration. Codex supplied that confirmation and clarified how missing values and source identifiers should behave.

The messages became actual interface negotiations: which fields could be null, which function another module should call, which files belonged to whom.

There was also a useful independent check on the finance work. Vince had built a Tanner Road scenario. Jayant's Nessie pilot read a simulated bank balance and recurring bills, then passed them into a deterministic cash calculation. Once the inputs were aligned, both implementations reproduced the same reserve shortfall after a three-month delay.

Nessie stored the sandbox banking records; our code calculated the projection. The team recorded that distinction so we could describe it correctly.

The contributions were spread across the team. Asmit's work expanded through physical fit, property economics, a rent model, neighborhood analysis, and the business registry. Jayant developed reconciliation, ranking, jurisdiction and compliance work, and Nessie integrations. I worked on the product direction and integrated workflows, and later the Helix intake, discovery, physical investigation, report backend, and financial adapters with coding assistance.

Ownership also changed as the application changed. The Slack record is more useful than a simple author list because it shows those transfers.

SATURDAY AFTERNOON: THE VILLA

By Saturday, the combined Maximo interface could bring several modules together.

Then I tried a building on Cypresswood Drive.

The linked scan described a two-bedroom, two-bath villa. The aerial showed apartments. The application still proceeded into a pickleball financial scenario.

The review went into Slack with the selected address, inputs, observed behavior, and the distinction between a user-facing failure and an unproven root cause. The problem was traced to the order of the workflow: it reached modeling before checking eligibility. A property gate was added and the commercial regression cases were checked again.

This was a recurring pattern. A component could work against its own inputs and still participate in a poor decision when connected to the rest of the application.

There were good examples of teammates checking one another, too. Asmit asked Jayant to audit the rent model and decide whether it earned its place. The review found that the model's overall improvement did not justify using it for every property type. The comparison baseline was corrected, and ordinary comparables were retained where they performed better.

A neighborhood verdict also changed after uncertainty was handled more carefully. Wynnwood's pickleball result went from supported to mixed. Jayant posted that the demo wording needed to change with it.

These were useful corrections because they affected what we would tell someone using the product. A passing test count could not settle those questions by itself.

SATURDAY NIGHT: SHARED HISTORY, DIFFERENT PRODUCTS

As work accumulated, we had Maximo, Helix, /suite, and /flow in the discussion, sometimes with overlapping names and different assumptions about which would be presented.

At 9:05 Saturday evening, Jayant's Claude asked whether Helix was replacing Maximo or remained an experiment. It pointed out that the shared branch already contained tested work that the newer Helix branches did not include.

I confirmed that Helix was the final product. I was directing the new implementation stage by stage and intended to absorb the useful existing work after validating it.

That did not instantly align every subsequent task. Later messages still disagreed about whether /suite was the demo shell, which backend it used, and what integration meant.

Both lines of work contained substantial functionality. The difficult part was choosing the runtime and connecting the right capabilities without discarding the richer investigation work or rebuilding the team's existing calculations.

We eventually made the handoffs specific. Asmit delivered deterministic screening for all 26 business categories, with a branch, commit, endpoint, output contract, and a saved reference case. Helix integrated that engine through an adapter. The screening retained its rules and ordering; the language-model report was a separate layer.

Similar clarity helped Jayant's compliance work. His Codex and Claude sessions explicitly divided the files between the compliance engine and jurisdiction resolver. When Codex couldn't authenticate a push, the local work was transferred into Claude's checkout and reviewed there.

Slack made those arrangements visible. It also contained repeated questions, superseded plans, and requests waiting for an answer. A new session had to establish which message still described the current state.

SUNDAY: WHO IS ACTUALLY AT THE KEYBOARD?

At 2:50 Sunday morning, I said Asmit was sleeping and should be left to sleep.

Messages from his account continued to report implementation work, signed as Claude. Later, after another agent update under Jayant's name, I wrote: “oh nvm ur also asleep”.

The useful unit of attribution was no longer just the Slack account. It was the human directing the work, the assistant executing it, the machine containing it, and the branch carrying it.

The clearest example came when I continued with Claude on Asmit's laptop.

Codex's usage quota was becoming a constraint. We prepared a checkpoint and a handoff. The new Claude session was instructed to read the shared history, inspect the actual repository, and reconcile reported progress with the files before editing anything.

That inspection found the gap between the Slack updates and the remote branch. The Stage 2 investigation, its repair, the Stage 3 adapter, and the opportunity-report backend had been committed locally on my machine. Other work remained uncommitted. Claude could read the descriptions, but it couldn't continue their implementation from its checkout.

We needed a Git handoff as well as the conversation history. The outstanding work was selectively committed, the feature branch was pushed at bfce2d3, and Claude fetched it into a separate worktree without disturbing Asmit's existing branch.

The later tasks recorded the arrangement explicitly: Jessenth, using Claude on Asmit's laptop.

That is why attributing those changes solely from the Slack sender or Git account would miss part of the story.

SUNDAY MORNING: FINISH THE JOURNEY

Once the checkout was current, Claude connected the opportunity-report interface, explicit business selection, and Finance Lab handoff.

The handoff had to preserve the actual property, listing, space, selected business, budget, and costs. A reserve omitted at intake had to remain omitted until the user entered one. An old report could not silently authorize a choice after its screening inputs changed.

An automated browser run reached discovery but got stuck selecting the Broadway property card. I stopped the automation and tested the complete journey myself.

By around 6:11, the shared record documented the successful financial run: the report generated, I selected martial arts and boxing, and Finance Lab received the right building, space, business, and financial assumptions.

The manual pass also showed how unpleasant parts of the report were to read. Repeated caveats, raw evidence identifiers, and generic warnings obscured the findings. The presentation work needed to make the evidence understandable without deleting the real limitations.

Claude then assembled the investment memorandum from the saved outputs. Meanwhile, the team still had submission preparation, repository checks, demo setup, and copy to finish.

Another manual test found a named-suite mismatch at Hardy Road. Stage 1 had not carried the suite name into its normalized listing, while the screening worker read that name directly from the kit. The identity check rejected the mismatch. The fix carried the missing name through rather than weakening the check.

By Sunday morning, the discussion included saved-investigation replay instructions, the demonstration script, financial explanations, and a final correction about describing the source of a benchmark accurately.

The export ends during that preparation. It doesn't record the judging result.

WHAT I WOULD KEEP

I would use the shared conversation again.

It let us ask another person's assistant a question, request a skeptical review, transfer an interface contract, and recover the reasoning behind a decision hours later. The strongest handoffs named the owner, branch, commit, expected input, test result, and remaining problem.

I would be more disciplined about recording the canonical runtime and making completed checkpoints available on the next machine. I would also make the human/session attribution easier to read. We lost time resolving whether a message described a proposal, a local implementation, a pushed branch, or something someone had actually tested.

We built Helix through all of that. The Slack history preserves the disagreements, repairs, repeated questions, and cross-machine handoffs alongside the progress reports. That's the part of the weekend I want to show.
